 [MuMiN](https://dl.acm.org/doi/epdf/10.1145/3477495.3531744) is a multilingual and multidimensional dataset that provides both fake and real news related to Covid-19 along with their social context in 6 different languages.
 Link to their github repository: [bigheiniu/MM-COVID: Cross Linugual COVID-19 Fake News Dataset](https://github.com/bigheiniu/MM-COVID?tab=readme-ov-file) 

### ~={pink}**Data collection process**=~
![[Pasted image 20260310141113.png]]

##### ==***News content collection:***==
1. Gather *reliable fact-checking evaluation results ~={pink}(real or fake)=~ from expert ~={pink}fact-checking websites=~* (Snopes & Poynter) in 6 languages *(English, Spanish, Portuguese, Hindi, French, Italian).*
2. For *each label (Fake) extracted from the fact-checking article* (those websites mostly fact-check fake news), we look for the ~={pink}original source URLs=~ of the fake news content. If not found (either in the website or in the archive), we assume the claim is the fake news itself.
3. Since *most fact-checking websites mostly care for fake news*, there's the need to bring balance with **real trustworthy news** brought by **official health websites** WHO or CDC, including only articles with the keywords: **"COVID-19", "Coronavirus", or "SARS-CoV-2"** in the title.
4. Using a tool called *Newspaper3k*, the ~={pink}textual content=~ and ~={pink}meta information=~ from the collected source URLs. The **sources of these news** are *social media posts, news agencies and blogs.*

##### ==***User Social Engagement collection***==
1. The user social engagements ae collected from the social platform based on the news content, using *Twitter advanced search API* [^1]**(via twarc)**.
2. Precisely, the **search query** is formed based on the ~={pink}URL=~, ~={pink}headline=~ and ~={pink}first sentence of the source content=~. The query is refined by omitting special characters and negative words and using TF-IDF to extract important words. Lastly, the query is **checked manually**.
3. The social engagements include *news-related tweets* and *their corresponding replies and retweets*

[^1]: twarc is a command line tool and Python library for collecting and archiving Twitter JSON data via the Twitter API. **twarc is no longer actively supported after changes to Twitter's API quotas made it unusable.**

##### ==***News content analysis:***==
- **Word Frequency and Text Clues:** The researchers visualized the most frequent words using word clouds to compare fake and real news across languages. They translated non-English tokens to English for comparison, discovering that fake news frequently mentioned medical words (doctor, hospital, vaccine) and specific countries, whereas real news focused on words like "test" and "patient".
- **Semantic Representation Visualization:** To understand the semantic differences between fake and real news, they used a multi-lingual model (RoBERTa) to learn the hidden representations of the text. They then applied a technique called t-SNE to visualize these representations and identify clusters of spreadable fake and real news.

##### ***==Language Spatiotemporal Analysis==***

- **Tracking Spreading and Debunking Timelines:** The researchers mapped out timelines to track how specific pieces of fake news originated, spread across different languages, and were eventually debunked. This process revealed delays in propagation (e.g., taking half a month for a fake claim to transit from English to Hindi) and showed that multiple versions of the same fake news often existed within a single language.

##### *==**Measuring Social Context**==*

- **Analysing User Network Structures:** The researchers compared the network connections of users by visualizing the follower and friends counts of individuals who engaged with fake versus real news.
- **Bot Detection:** To assess the likelihood of automated bot involvement, they sampled 500 users who interacted with fake news and 500 who interacted with real news for each language. They ran these profiles through a state-of-the-art tool called **Botometer** to calculate cumulative bot-likelihood distributions.
- **Sentiment Analysis via Emojis:** Because standard sentiment classifiers don't cover all six languages, the researchers analysed the distribution of emojis in tweets and replies as a proxy for user emotion. They found that fake news replies contained more emotional emojis (like laughing or angry faces), while real news tweets were dominated by directional and enumerative emojis.
- **Interaction Intensity Measurement:** They compared the accumulation of retweets and replies to gauge how intensely users interacted with fake news versus real news across different languages.
- **Topic Analysis via Hashtags:** To pinpoint topic differences in Twitter conversations, they filtered out ubiquitous tags (like #COVID-19 or #Coronavirus) and extracted the most salient hashtags. This revealed that fake news tweets often highlighted conspiracy theories (#5g, #hydroxychloroquine) or political figures, while real news tweets favoured official health agencies or general prevention advice.

##### *==**Temporal Information Analysis**==*

- **Social Engagement Patterns Over Time:** The researchers analysed the temporal patterns of social interactions by plotting the volume of engagements over time. They compared these timelines to identify characteristic patterns, such as sudden spikes in engagement for fake news versus steady increases for real news.
